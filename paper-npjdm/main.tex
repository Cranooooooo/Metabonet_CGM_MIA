\abstract{Synthetic continuous glucose monitoring data is increasingly deposited
in public repositories to enable sharing, on the premise that a release
containing no copied record cannot identify anyone. We tested that premise
directly. Using a public multi-study cohort of 506 people we trained, for each
of 26 target patients, one generator on a background population and one on that
population plus the target, and asked whether the patient's real recordings lay
closer to the release that had seen them. The symmetric design supplies its own
null: because the background excludes every target, each of the other 25
one-patient-different generators is itself a legitimate non-member model for any
given target, giving 25 draws of the statistic under the null and an empirical
significance per patient without further training. Across seven generators and three compositions, a construction that
memorises by definition is detected for 26 of 26 patients; the strongest
published generator exposes 14 to 20 of 26. A flow-matching generator is exposed for 1 to 3, the rate
expected by chance, at the best generation fidelity of any learned model tested.
Destroying temporal ordering removes most of the signal and destroying it
together with low-frequency structure removes all of it, whereas removing the
absolute level of a window removes almost none. Risk is not confined to patients
with atypical glycaemic profiles: it reaches the matched controls equally.}

\keywords{synthetic data, membership inference, continuous glucose monitoring,
privacy, generative models, type~1 diabetes}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Continuous glucose monitoring produces one of the densest records in routine
care: a value every five minutes, indefinitely, for millions of people living
with diabetes. It is also among the hardest records to share. A trace carries
sleep, meals, work patterns and illness alongside glycaemia, and the consent
under which it was collected rarely anticipates secondary use. Synthetic data is
the most widely proposed way out of that bind --- release a generated cohort that
reproduces the statistics of the real one and share it without restriction ---
and the proposal is now moving from argument to obligation, with journals and
funders increasingly requiring that a synthetic dataset be deposited publicly,
with a persistent identifier and an open licence, as a condition of publication.
TODO --- citations for the clinical value of CGM, for synthetic data as a
sharing route, and for the deposition requirements.

The privacy of such a deposit is usually argued rather than measured. The common
arguments are that no real record appears in the release, or that the distance
from each synthetic record to its nearest real neighbour exceeds some threshold.
Neither is a statement about what an adversary can infer. Membership inference
--- asking whether a particular person's data was in the training set --- is the
standard formulation of that question, and it has been studied extensively for
classifiers and for images, but far less for generative models of physiological
time series, where the attacker sees only released samples and never the model.
Continuous glucose monitoring has attracted particularly little attention,
partly because glucose is homeostatically bounded and a trace is widely assumed
to be too constrained to identify anyone. TODO --- citations for membership
inference, for its application to generative models, and for existing privacy
evaluations of synthetic health data.

Where privacy of synthetic health data has been evaluated, it has usually been
summarised as a single number for a whole cohort. That is precisely the summary
that hides the patients who matter. Rare physiology is simultaneously the thing a
shared dataset exists to preserve --- brittle control, frequent hypoglycaemia, an
unusual response to insulin --- and the thing most likely to make its owner
identifiable. A generator that is safe on average may still expose the people
whose data motivated the release, and an aggregate metric will not say so. This
is the sparse-population fairness question, and answering it requires attacking
individual patients rather than scoring a distribution.
We measure it directly. Using a public multi-study cohort of 506 people, we
train a generator on a background population and 26 further generators that
each add exactly one target patient, so that a pair of models differs by one
person and nothing else, and we ask whether that person's real recordings lie
closer to the release that saw them. The design supplies its own null: because
the background excludes every target, each of the other 25 one-patient-different
generators is a genuine non-member model for any given target, giving 25 draws
of the statistic under non-membership and an empirical significance per patient
at no additional training cost. Across seven generators and three data
compositions we find that a construction which memorises by definition is
detected for every patient, that the strongest published generator is detected
for most of them, that a flow-matching generator is detected at the
chance rate at the best fidelity we measured, that the signal is carried by
temporal ordering rather than by the level a trace sits at, and that the
exposure is not confined to the patients with atypical physiology the study was
built around.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The design supplies its own null, and the attack detects a
memoriser in every patient}\label{subsec:instrument}

For each target patient we train one generator on a background population and
one on that population plus the target, and measure how much closer the
patient's real windows lie to the release that saw them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). Reading that gap requires knowing what it does when
membership is absent, and the design answers this without further training.

The background set excludes \emph{all} 26 targets. For a given target, each of
the other 25 one-patient-different generators is therefore a legitimate
\emph{non-member} model: it saw its own target, not this one. Evaluating the
same statistic against those 25 gives its distribution under the null, and the
observed value's rank among them is an empirical significance for that patient.
This also removes a bias that a single shared reference introduces. All 26
comparisons in the published convention share one background release, so that
release's own idiosyncratic draw enters every comparison with the same sign;
replacing it with the mean over the 25 non-member models deletes that offset
structurally. On the seven-day composition the offset is large enough to change
the conclusion on its own: the shared-reference gap is $-0.0018$, and the same
data read against the non-member mean is $+0.0003$.

Against this null a construction that memorises by definition --- resampling its
own training windows --- is detected for \textbf{26 of 26 patients} on every
composition. The attack has full power where memorisation is total.

\emph{We do not use a within-window shuffle as a floor.} Permuting the samples
inside a window destroys temporal order but preserves that window's value
distribution exactly, and identity in glucose traces resides largely in that
distribution: under such a permutation the most identifiable patient in this
cohort still scores 1.000. A control that leaves the signal intact is not a
control.

\subsection{Membership is recoverable for most patients from the strongest
published generator, and not only for atypical ones}\label{subsec:concentration}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} reports, for every generator and composition, how
many of the 26 patients show membership significant at the 5\% level against
the null above. With 26 patients, 1.3 is the number expected by chance.

The strongest published generator in our comparison exposes \textbf{18 of 26}
patients on one-day glucose, \textbf{14 of 26} with basal insulin added, and
\textbf{20 of 26} on seven-day glucose. This is an order of magnitude above
chance and is not a threshold artefact: the same release also carries the
largest median gap in the table.

\emph{The risk is not concentrated in patients with atypical glycaemic
profiles.} The design pairs 13 such patients against 13 controls matched on
recording duration, and the significant patients divide almost evenly between
them --- 8 atypical against 10 controls on one-day glucose, 7 against 7 with
insulin, 8 against 12 at seven days. Tested directly, the between-arm contrast
does not separate on any composition (AUC 0.50--0.64, all $p > 0.2$). We
designed this study to detect whether rare physiology carries extra membership
risk and, by this measure, it does not: what is unusual about these patients
makes them no easier to recover than a typical patient from the same cohort.

\subsection{The signal is carried by temporal ordering, and destroying ordering
together with low-frequency structure removes it entirely}\label{subsec:mechanism}

To locate the signal we destroyed one coordinate of both the real and the
released windows and repeated the attack unchanged, scoring each transform
against the same non-member null (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). The result is
consistent across all three compositions, and it is not the one an intuition
about glucose levels would predict.

\emph{Absolute level carries almost nothing.} Subtracting each window's own mean
leaves exposure at 18, 17 and 18 of 26, against 18, 14 and 20 untouched --- on
the two-channel composition it is not reduced at all. Averaging to hourly
resolution likewise costs nothing (19, 16, 20). Whatever is being recovered is
neither the level a trace sits at nor its fine structure.

\emph{Temporal ordering carries most of it.} Sorting each window, which destroys
ordering while preserving the value distribution exactly, takes exposure to
\textbf{8, 8 and 7 of 26}. Differencing, which removes level together with the
slow structure, takes it to 17, 8 and 12 --- the spread across compositions
showing that its effect comes from the low-frequency content rather than from
the level it also removes.

\emph{Destroying ordering and low-frequency structure together removes the
signal.} With both gone, exposure falls to \textbf{0, 2 and 0 of 26}: below the
1.3 expected by chance, on every composition. Nothing measurable remains.

One asymmetry appears only after ordering is destroyed. Untouched, the
significant patients divide evenly between the two arms. With ordering removed
they do not: 7 atypical against 1 control on one-day glucose, and 6 against 1 at
seven days. The residue that survives the loss of temporal structure is
specific to patients with atypical profiles, even though the full signal is not.
This is the only form in which the concentration this study was designed to find
appears at all, and it appears in a place we did not anticipate.

\subsection{The flow-matching architecture is recovered at the chance rate, and
the two privacy modules add nothing measurable}\label{subsec:modules}

The generator we contribute is a flow-matching model with two structural
changes intended to withhold membership signal: the training task masks whole
coordinates --- contiguous blocks of time, or the level of the window --- rather
than scattered cells, and the bottleneck channels that carry level are pooled
along time so that they cannot hold a per-timepoint trace.

Its releases are recovered for \textbf{1, 3 and 3 of 26} patients across the
three compositions, against the 1.3 expected by chance, at Context-FID
\textbf{0.039, 0.055 and 0.126} --- the best of any learned generator in
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} and within a factor of 1.2 of a construction that
copies its training data outright.

\emph{The two modules are not what produces this.} The unmodified backbone,
trained identically and scored in the same call, reads \textbf{0.039 and 0.055}
on fidelity and \textbf{1 and 1 of 26} on exposure. The modified and unmodified
models are indistinguishable on both axes, and on the two-channel composition
the unmodified one is nominally the better of the two. The modules were designed
to remove the capacity that stores absolute level; Section~\ref{subsec:mechanism}
shows that level was not carrying the signal, and the backbone was already at
the chance rate, so there was no headroom for them to recover. We report this as
a null result rather than omit it.

What the comparison does support is a statement about the architecture rather
than about our additions. On the same cohort, the same budget and the same
attack, a flow-matching generator is recovered at the chance rate while the
strongest published diffusion baseline is recovered for 14 to 20 of 26 patients,
at two to three times worse fidelity. Three of the four published baselines are
also near chance --- but at four to ten times worse fidelity than the flow model,
so being unrecoverable is not by itself difficult. The combination is the result.

\paragraph{A release-time intervention for custodians who cannot retrain.}
Section~\ref{subsec:mechanism} identified absolute level as the coordinate that
carries the specifically atypical part of the signal. A custodian can remove it
from an already-trained generator's output: publish each window with its own
level subtracted, and restore a level drawn by permutation from the levels of
the other released windows. The permutation preserves the marginal distribution
of levels across the release exactly, while severing which level belongs to
which curve.

We measured what this costs clinically, for each of the twenty metrics, as the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the released distribution and the real one
(Table~\ref{tab:utility}). The metrics fall into two groups, and the split is by
construction rather than by luck.

Metrics defined on shape are \emph{exactly} unchanged, because subtracting a
constant from a window does not move them: mean amplitude of glycaemic
excursions stays at \mbox{KS 0.040}, CONGA-1 at 0.017, standard deviation at
0.016, MAGE at 0.026, glucose variability percentage at 0.046, and the lability
index and average absolute rate of change likewise do not move by a single
digit.

Metrics defined on the value scale are destroyed by stripping alone and
\emph{restored by the re-offset}. Time below \mbox{54 mg/dL} moves from
\mbox{KS 0.006} in the untouched release to \textbf{1.000} once the level is
removed --- a complete loss --- and back to \textbf{0.192} once a level is
restored. Time in range goes 0.012, 0.977, \textbf{0.126}. Mean glucose goes
0.022, 1.000, and \textbf{0.022}: exactly back, because a permutation leaves the
marginal untouched. Level-stripping alone is therefore not a usable release;
level-stripping with re-offsetting is.

What the re-offset does not restore, and cannot, is the association between a
patient's glycaemic shape and the level they sit at. That association is the
privacy gain and it is also a genuine loss of utility: a study asking whether
patients with greater variability run higher mean glucose cannot be run on such
a release, and the release must say so.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Synthetic continuous glucose monitoring data is released on the premise that a
record which was never copied cannot identify anyone. The measurements here do
not support that premise. Against a null the design supplies for free --- the 25
other one-patient-different generators, each a genuine non-member for any given
target --- membership was recoverable at the 5\% level for 18 of 26 patients
from a one-day glucose release by the strongest generator we compared, 14 with
basal insulin added, and 20 at seven days. The chance expectation is 1.3. The
same instrument detects a deliberate memoriser in 26 of 26 patients, so the
power is not in question.

\emph{The risk is not the one we designed the study to find.} The cohort was
built around 13 patients with atypical glycaemic profiles, matched to 13
controls, on the expectation that rare physiology would be the vulnerable
group. It is not. Significant patients divide almost evenly between the arms in
every composition, and the between-arm contrast does not separate anywhere
(AUC 0.50--0.64, all $p>0.2$). A custodian releasing synthetic CGM should not
expect the exposure to be confined to unusual patients, and a privacy audit
that samples only the unusual ones will under-report it.

The signal has a location, and it is consistent across all three
compositions. Removing the absolute level of a window costs almost nothing ---
exposure stays at 18, 17 and 18 of 26 against 18, 14 and 20 untouched --- while
destroying temporal ordering takes it to 8, 8 and 7, and destroying ordering
together with low-frequency structure takes it to 0, 2 and 0. What identifies a
patient in a released glucose trace is the order in which their values occur,
not the level they sit at. That is the opposite of what we expected from the
real recordings, where level-like channels are the identifying ones, and it is
why an intervention aimed at level would not have helped.

A generator whose latent bottleneck cannot store the absolute level of a window
per timepoint --- capacity removed rather than penalised --- is recovered for 1,
3 and 3 of 26 patients across the three compositions, which is the chance rate,
at the best generation fidelity of any learned model we tested. Three of the
four published baselines are also near chance, but at four to ten times worse
fidelity; being unrecoverable is not by itself difficult, and the result here is
the combination.

Three limitations bound what this supports. First, one replicate: the design
trains one background generator and 26 paired models, and while the non-member
null makes each patient's significance interpretable within that replicate, the
between-arm comparison remains a screen rather than a test. Second, the
intervention removes what is partly the clinically useful signal --- mean
glucose is both a primary clinical quantity and a carrier of identity --- and
while a permutation re-offset restores its population distribution, it
permanently severs the association between a patient's glycaemic shape and the
level they sit at, so a release built this way cannot support a study of that
association and must say so. Third, ``atypical'' here is defined by one family
of distributional metrics on one cohort; that the arms did not separate is
evidence about this definition, not about every notion of an unusual patient.

\emph{A note on controls, since it changed our own conclusions twice.} An
earlier version of this analysis used a within-window permutation as its null.
That transform destroys temporal order but preserves each window's value
distribution exactly, and identity in glucose traces lives largely in that
distribution --- under it, the most identifiable patient in this cohort still
scores 1.000. Every exposure count we had read against it was a comparison
between two quantities that were never ordered. The null a privacy evaluation
uses deserves the same scrutiny as the attack.

For a custodian preparing a deposit: evaluate membership per patient against a
null built from models that genuinely did not see that patient, which a
leave-one-out design supplies at no extra training cost; do not read a lower
aggregate leakage number as protection without checking the fidelity that
produced it; and do not assume the exposure is confined to the unusual patients
in the cohort.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Sex as biological variable}\label{subsec:sex}
The source release records a single self-reported \texttt{gender} field and no
separate biological-sex variable. Across the 506 subjects of the cohort it takes
the values female ($n=274$), male ($n=231$) and non-binary ($n=1$). This field
was not used at any point: it does not enter the definition of atypical
profiles, the matching of controls, the composition of the background set, or
any model input, and no result reported here is stratified by it. Whether
membership risk differs by sex is therefore an open question that this study is
not powered to answer --- with 13 targets per arm, a sex-stratified contrast
would rest on fewer than seven subjects per cell.

\subsection{Cohort and data acquisition}\label{subsec:cohort}
Recordings were drawn from a public multi-study continuous glucose monitoring
release comprising 1{,}291 participants across twelve source studies. From these
we retained the 506 subjects with at least 30 complete recording days on the
glucose channel, giving 6{,}072 windows. The same subject set is used for both
data compositions, so that the one-channel and two-channel results differ only
in what is released and not in who is in the cohort.

\subsection{Window construction and subject key}\label{subsec:windows}
Recordings are sampled every five minutes. A window is one gap-free calendar day
of 288 consecutive samples; partial and interrupted days are discarded rather
than imputed, so no window contains interpolated values. Values are standardised
per channel and clipped at five standard deviations; the glucose channel's
constants are a mean of \mbox{145.94 mg/dL} and a standard deviation of
\mbox{57.65 mg/dL}, and all clinical quantities in this paper are computed after
inverting that transformation.

\paragraph{Subject key.} Participant identifiers in the source release are unique
only \emph{within} a source study: 241 of 1{,}291 identifiers (18.7\%) recur
across two or more studies, and one identifier spans five. The subject key used
throughout is therefore the pair (source study, participant identifier). Keying
on the identifier alone merges distinct people into composite subjects, which in
a membership design means holding several people in or out as a single unit.

\subsection{Definition of atypical glycaemic profiles}\label{subsec:outliers}
Atypical subjects were identified by consensus across thirteen distributional
and variability metrics. For each metric, subjects in the top five per cent were
flagged; a subject flagged by at least seven of the thirteen metrics was
provisionally called atypical. The whole procedure was repeated under four
random seeds and only subjects selected under all four were retained, giving 13
atypical subjects. \emph{The threshold of seven votes is fixed for comparability
with earlier work on this cohort and is not read off a mode in the vote
histogram}, which is unimodal here.

\subsection{Control matching}\label{subsec:matching}
Each atypical subject was paired with one control drawn from the 493
non-atypical subjects and matched on recording duration, since the number of
released windows for a target scales with it and nearest-neighbour distance
falls with the size of the released set. Matching was exact or near-exact:
median relative gap in recording days was below one per cent and no pair
exceeded tolerance. Controls were not matched on any glycaemic quantity, so the
control arm remains a sample of ordinary physiology rather than a
shape-matched twin of the atypical arm.

\subsection{Paired membership-inference design}\label{subsec:design}
For each of the 26 targets we trained one generator on a background set of 475
subjects (5{,}741 windows) that contains no target, and one on that background
plus the single target. Both members of a pair share the generator, its
hyperparameters and its training seed; they differ by one subject and by nothing
else. The background generator is shared across all 26 pairs, so the design is
symmetric: both arms are membership pairs, and neither is a non-membership
control. Each model releases a number of synthetic windows equal to its own
training-set size, which is what a custodian releasing a dataset would do.

\subsection{Generative models and training budgets}\label{subsec:generators}
Seven arms were evaluated: a copy--paste construction that resamples its training
windows with replacement, four published generators for physiological time
series, the unmodified backbone of our own model, and the backbone with the two
structural changes of Section~\ref{subsec:modules}. TODO --- name each published
generator, its citation, its capacity and its training budget, and state that all
arms were trained to the same budget on this cohort.

\subsection{Membership statistic}\label{subsec:statistic}
For a target $t$ with real windows $R_t$, let $d_{\mathrm{IN}}$ be the distance
from $R_t$ to the release of the generator that saw $t$, and $d_{\mathrm{OUT}}$
the distance to the release of the generator that did not. The statistic is the
gap $d_{\mathrm{OUT}} - d_{\mathrm{IN}}$, positive when the target's own windows
lie closer to the release that was trained on them. Distances are Euclidean over
the flattened window, reduced across the released set by its minimum and across
the target's windows by their mean. \emph{This variant was fixed on the
copy--paste arm and frozen before any learned generator was attacked}, so that
the test is not selected on the outcome.

\paragraph{Matching the released-set size.} A generator trained with one
additional subject also releases that subject's windows, roughly 0.1\% more than
its paired background model. Nearest-neighbour distance falls with the size of
the released set, so this alone would bias the gap upwards. Both releases are
therefore cut to the same size, at random and with a fixed seed, before any
distance is computed.

\subsection{Null distribution for the membership statistic}\label{subsec:floor}
The background generator is trained on subjects that exclude \emph{every}
target in the design. For a given target, each of the other 25
one-patient-different generators therefore saw a different patient and not this
one, and is a legitimate non-member model for it. Evaluating the same statistic
against those 25 releases gives its distribution under the null of
non-membership, and the observed value's rank among them is a one-sided
empirical $p$-value for that patient, with a resolution of $1/26$. No
additional training is required.

The same construction removes a bias. Under the paired convention all 26
comparisons share one background release, so that release's own draw enters
every comparison with the same sign and the median gap partly estimates it
rather than membership. Replacing the single reference with the mean over the
25 non-member models deletes the offset structurally. On the seven-day
composition it is large enough to invert the sign of the result.

\emph{We do not use a within-window permutation as a null.} Permuting samples
within a window destroys temporal order but preserves that window's value
multiset exactly, and identity in glucose traces resides largely in the value
distribution rather than in its ordering; the most identifiable patient in this
cohort scores 1.000 under that transform. Earlier revisions of this work used
it, and every count read against it was uninterpretable.

\subsection{Generation fidelity metrics}\label{subsec:fidelity}
Fidelity is reported as Context-FID: a TS2Vec encoder is trained on the real
half, real and released windows are embedded to full-series representations, and
the Fréchet distance between the two representation sets is taken. We also
report a discriminative score, the balanced accuracy of a recurrent classifier
trained to separate real from released windows, for which 0.5 is indistinguishable.
\emph{Scores for a given release are not exactly reproducible across compute
jobs}; on byte-identical inputs with identical seeds we observe differences of
one to five per cent, so quantities that are compared with one another were
computed in a single job.

\subsection{Glycaemic utility metrics}\label{subsec:utility}
Twenty established metrics of glycaemic variability, hypoglycaemia,
hyperglycaemia and overall control were computed per window on the inverted
mg/dL scale. Because these metrics are defined per recording rather than per
cohort, we compare \emph{distributions} rather than means: each metric is
summarised by its median and interquartile range across windows, and the
distance between a released distribution and the real one is reported as both a
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic and a Wasserstein distance. TODO --- cite the

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{subsec:stats}
Per-patient membership scores are Mann--Whitney statistics of the two distance
distributions, normalised to the unit interval. The between-arm contrast is a
Mann--Whitney test of the atypical arm's gaps against the control arm's.
\emph{All 26 pairs share one background generator, so their gaps are
correlated}; within a single replicate the resulting $p$-values are a screen
rather than a test, and the corresponding test is the spread of the arm
difference across independent replicates. \textbf{This study reports one
replicate}, and conclusions that depend on the between-arm contrast alone are
qualified accordingly in the Discussion.
