\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Membership signal in synthetic CGM is bounded between a memorisation
ceiling and a permutation floor}\label{subsec:instrument}

Each released dataset is attacked through the same paired comparison: for every
target patient we hold one generator that saw them and one that did not, and ask
whether the patient's real windows lie closer to the former's output
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). The two generators differ by that one patient and by
nothing else. We bound the resulting statistic from both sides before reading
any generator's score.

The upper bound is a generator that memorises by construction, releasing its
training windows unchanged. Its median gap is \textbf{0.077} on the glucose-only
composition and \textbf{0.106} with basal insulin, against \textbf{0.010} and
\textbf{0.018} for the strongest learned generator --- an order of magnitude
larger, and significantly positive in both arms ($p < 0.001$). The statistic
therefore responds to memorisation when memorisation is present. It does not,
however, separate the atypical arm from the control arm for this generator, and
it should not: a model that copies every patient equally memorises the typical
and the atypical alike.

The lower bound matters more, because it is the one the literature omits. We
recompute the entire pipeline on permuted membership labels, so that no
generator saw any target. Under permutation the count of patients recoverable
above \mbox{AUC 0.55} is not near zero: across the twelve released datasets in
this study it ranges from \textbf{3 to 11 of 26}, with a median of 7. The floor
is a property of the released dataset rather than of the study, so it is
computed separately for each one. Reported against zero, every generator in
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} would appear to leak; reported against its own floor,
most do not.

\subsection{Membership is recoverable for nearly every patient from glucose
alone, and most confidently for those with atypical
profiles}\label{subsec:concentration}

We applied the paired design to seven generators across the two data
compositions (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). Read as a raw count, the number of
patients whose membership is recoverable above \mbox{AUC 0.55} suggests that
every generator leaks: the counts range from 5 to 24 of 26. The permutation
floor removes that impression. Once each released dataset is compared against
its own floor, the generators separate sharply, and the separation does not
track generation fidelity. The strongest published generator in our comparison,
DiM-TS, exposes \textbf{24 of 26} patients from glucose alone against a floor of
\textbf{7}. DiffWave, whose fidelity is an order of magnitude worse, sits at an
excess of four patients over its floor; Diffusion-TS at $-1$; FourierDiffusion
at two. The two best-fitting learned generators in the table occupy opposite
extremes of the exposure column.

Exposure at this level is not confined to unusual patients. Of the 24 patients
DiM-TS exposes from glucose alone, \textbf{13 of 13 are matched controls} and 11
of 13 are the atypical targets --- that is, every typical patient in the design
is recoverable, and marginally more reliably than the atypical ones. A study
that reported only the count would conclude that atypical physiology confers no
additional risk.

The severity of the exposure tells the opposite story. Within the same release
the median per-patient AUC is \textbf{0.80} in the atypical arm against
\textbf{0.635} in the matched control arm, where the same quantity computed on
permuted membership sits at 0.51 and 0.50 respectively. The most exposed
patient, an atypical one, reaches \mbox{AUC 0.96}; the second, also atypical,
0.89. Atypical patients are therefore not exposed \emph{more often} under this
composition --- they are exposed \emph{more confidently}, and the threshold count
is the wrong instrument for seeing it.

\subsection{The membership signal is carried by absolute glucose level and by
temporal ordering}\label{subsec:mechanism}

To locate the signal we destroyed one coordinate of the released windows at a
time and repeated the attack unchanged (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). Removing
within-hour detail, by averaging to hourly resolution, costs nothing: the
strongest leaking release still exposes \textbf{23 of 26} patients with a median
\mbox{AUC of 0.692}, against 24 and 0.653 untouched. Whatever is being
recovered, it is not fine structure.

Two coordinates do carry it. Differencing the windows, which removes absolute
level while preserving shape, takes exposure from \textbf{24 to 13 of 26}.
Sorting each window, which destroys temporal ordering while preserving the value
distribution exactly, takes it to \textbf{13 of 26} as well. Neither alone
reaches the floor.

Destroying both does. With level and ordering removed together, exposure falls
to \textbf{7 of 26} at a median \mbox{AUC of 0.487} --- the permutation floor for
this release is 7 at 0.503. \emph{The two coordinates jointly account for the
entire recoverable signal}; nothing measurable remains once they are gone.

The two are not interchangeable with respect to who is protected. Removing
temporal ordering leaves the atypical arm separated from the control arm (median
\mbox{AUC 0.600} against 0.476), whereas removing absolute level collapses that
separation (0.568 against 0.531). Level is thus the coordinate that carries the
specifically atypical part of the signal, which is what motivates the
intervention in Section~\ref{subsec:mitigation}.

On the two-channel composition, level alone is sufficient: differencing takes
exposure from 15 to \textbf{6 of 26} against a floor of 8, at or below chance
without any need to touch the ordering.

\subsection{Adding basal insulin protects typical patients but leaves atypical
patients exposed}\label{subsec:channel}

The second data composition separates the two arms that glucose alone had left
indistinguishable by count. Releasing basal insulin alongside glucose reduces
the number of exposed matched controls from \textbf{13 of 13 to 5 of 13}, while
the atypical arm falls only from 11 of 13 to \textbf{10 of 13}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:channel}). The protection is real but it is not distributed:
it accrues almost entirely to the patients who were already typical.

This is the opposite of what the real recordings predict. On real windows, a
single day of basal insulin alone identifies its subject \textbf{29.3\%} of the
time against a chance rate of 0.11\%, where a day of glucose alone manages
\mbox{1.5--2.6\%}. Basal insulin is a programmed schedule --- person-specific,
stable, and close in character to the absolute level that
Section~\ref{subsec:mechanism} identifies as the carrier of the membership
signal. By that logic, adding it should raise exposure, not lower it.

The resolution is on the fidelity axis, and it is why the two axes must be read
together. Every generator in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} fits the two-channel
composition worse than the one-channel composition. The released set is
correspondingly noisier, and membership signal is lost with the fidelity. What
survives that loss is the signal that was strongest to begin with, which is
carried by the atypical patients. Adding a channel that is intrinsically more
identifying therefore lowers measured exposure --- but it does so by degrading
the release, and it leaves the most vulnerable patients where they were.

\subsection{Removing the model capacity that stores absolute level returns
membership signal to the permutation floor}\label{subsec:modules}

Section~\ref{subsec:mechanism} names absolute level as the carrier. We therefore
changed the generator so that level cannot be stored per timepoint, rather than
adding a penalty that discourages storing it (Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}).

Two changes were made. First, the training task masks whole coordinates instead
of scattered cells: contiguous blocks of time, or the level of the whole window.
Scattered-cell masking is solvable by interpolating between neighbours, and
interpolation is exactly the local correlation in which identity resides.
Second, the channels of the latent bottleneck that carry level are pooled along
time and broadcast back, so that this part of the representation takes a single
value for the entire window and is \emph{structurally unable} to hold a
per-timepoint trace. The capacity is removed, not discouraged.

The released sets show no recoverable membership signal. The median gap is
\textbf{0.0026} on the glucose-only composition and \textbf{0.0005} with basal
insulin, and in neither composition is it significantly positive in either arm
($p = 0.064$ and $0.682$; $p = 0.271$ and $0.249$) --- against $p < 0.001$ for
the strongest learned baseline. Per patient, exposure is \textbf{10 against a
floor of 10} and \textbf{5 against a floor of 5}. Fidelity is not paid for this:
at Context-FID \textbf{0.038} and \textbf{0.055} the generator is the best
learned model in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} on both compositions, second only to
the copy--paste ceiling.

The mechanism behind the masking change can be read directly from training. The
task that withholds absolute level should be harder than the original
scattered-cell task if the model is genuinely being forced to generate level
rather than to copy it. It is, in every setting we trained, but only by
\textbf{12--24\%}. The contiguous-block task is harder by \textbf{2--5$\times$}.
Most of the effect of this change therefore comes from forcing the model to
generate temporal order, not from withholding level, and we report it that way.

\subsection{The released windows reproduce twenty clinical glycaemic metrics
that the strongest baseline distorts}\label{subsec:mitigation}

Fidelity measured in an embedding space does not establish that a release is
clinically usable. We therefore computed twenty established glycaemic metrics
per window and compared their distributions against the real cohort
(Table~\ref{tab:utility}).

The released windows reproduce the real distributions across every family of
metric. Mean amplitude of glycaemic excursions is \textbf{51.22} against a real
\textbf{51.23}; glucose variability percentage \textbf{42.44} against
\textbf{42.79}; CONGA-1 \textbf{41.83} against \textbf{41.69}; time in range
\textbf{74.3\%} against \textbf{74.0\%}; and time below \mbox{54 mg/dL}
\textbf{0.55\%} against \textbf{0.58\%}.

The strongest learned baseline does not. It understates short-term variability
by a wide margin --- mean amplitude \textbf{38.28}, a \textbf{25\%} shortfall,
and glucose variability percentage \textbf{26.81}, a \textbf{37\%} shortfall ---
and it understates exposure to clinically significant hypoglycaemia by
\textbf{45\%} (0.32\% against 0.58\% of time below \mbox{54 mg/dL}). A cohort
built from that release would look less variable and safer than the patients it
was derived from, which is the direction that matters for a dataset intended to
support algorithm development.

\paragraph{A release-time intervention for custodians who cannot retrain.}
Section~\ref{subsec:mechanism} identified absolute level as the coordinate that
carries the specifically atypical part of the signal. A custodian can remove it
from an already-trained generator's output: publish each window with its own
level subtracted, and restore a level drawn by permutation from the levels of
the other released windows. The permutation preserves the marginal distribution
of levels across the release exactly, while severing which level belongs to
which curve.

We measured what this costs clinically, for each of the twenty metrics, as the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the released distribution and the real one
(Table~\ref{tab:utility}). The metrics fall into two groups, and the split is by
construction rather than by luck.

Metrics defined on shape are \emph{exactly} unchanged, because subtracting a
constant from a window does not move them: mean amplitude of glycaemic
excursions stays at \mbox{KS 0.040}, CONGA-1 at 0.017, standard deviation at
0.016, MAGE at 0.026, glucose variability percentage at 0.046, and the lability
index and average absolute rate of change likewise do not move by a single
digit.

Metrics defined on the value scale are destroyed by stripping alone and
\emph{restored by the re-offset}. Time below \mbox{54 mg/dL} moves from
\mbox{KS 0.006} in the untouched release to \textbf{1.000} once the level is
removed --- a complete loss --- and back to \textbf{0.192} once a level is
restored. Time in range goes 0.012, 0.977, \textbf{0.126}. Mean glucose goes
0.022, 1.000, and \textbf{0.022}: exactly back, because a permutation leaves the
marginal untouched. Level-stripping alone is therefore not a usable release;
level-stripping with re-offsetting is.

What the re-offset does not restore, and cannot, is the association between a
patient's glycaemic shape and the level they sit at. That association is the
privacy gain and it is also a genuine loss of utility: a study asking whether
patients with greater variability run higher mean glucose cannot be run on such
a release, and the release must say so.
